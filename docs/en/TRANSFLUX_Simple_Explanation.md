# What is TRANSFLUX? A plain-language explanation

Picture an ordinary computer, or an app on your phone. They work like strict, uncompromising accountants. For them the world is ideal: everything can be measured to the millimetre, everything happens instantly, and they believe the space around them is completely safe.

You and I know real life is different. A scale can be a little off. A mood changes gradually. Sometimes it matters more to get out alive than to fill in the paperwork neatly.

**TRANSFLUX** is a new way of building programs. It is an attempt to teach computers to think less like soulless calculators and closer to the way we, living people, think.

In short, TRANSFLUX teaches computers three things: **to doubt**, **to remember the past**, and **to survive**.

Here is what that looks like in ordinary life.

---

## 1. Honesty instead of overconfidence (knowing how to doubt)

**How an ordinary computer thinks:**
It is 100% sure. If the kitchen thermometer says 80 degrees, the computer treats that as exactly 80.000 degrees. It does not consider that the thermometer is old, cheap, and can be wrong.

**How TRANSFLUX thinks:**
It is honest. It says: *"The temperature is about 80 degrees, but I am only 80% sure, because the thermometer is unreliable."*
If a recipe asks you to mix two ingredients and both weights are only approximate, TRANSFLUX will not pretend it knows the exact total. It carries the doubt forward: *"We have about a kilogram of dough, give or take 100 grams."*
In medicine or in an autopilot, that honesty can save a life. A doctor is better off knowing the device is *not sure* of a diagnosis than receiving a wrong answer that looks absolutely "certain".

---

## 2. Smooth change instead of jumps (memory and habit)

**How an ordinary computer thinks:**
It lives only in the current moment. Its variables are just boxes. If a child weighed 20 kg yesterday and the scale says 50 kg today, an ordinary computer simply writes "weight = 50 kg". It does not care that this cannot happen.

**How TRANSFLUX thinks:**
It treats data as a river that flows. It remembers the "history" and knows how fast things are changing. If the weight jumps from 20 to 50 kg in one day, TRANSFLUX understands: *"Stop. That jump is too sharp. That is not natural."* It treats this as a "trauma" or a fault and raises an alarm (for example, it asks you to check the scale again). It understands that everything has inertia — water does not boil in an instant, and people do not lose weight in a second.

---

## 3. Survival instead of blind preservation (an instinct for self-preservation)

**How an ordinary computer thinks:**
Its main goal is to delete nothing and to save everything neatly. If an intruder is breaking into your apartment (or into the system), an ordinary computer will keep writing data into a file until the last moment, so that later it is convenient for the thieves to read it.

**How TRANSFLUX thinks:**
For it, **survival and safety** come first, not mere preservation of data.
It has a built-in sense of threat. If it understands that the situation is getting out of control (the data is too doubtful, or strange sharp jumps keep happening), a survival instinct fires — "panic".
This is not a tantrum. It is a clear plan: it can instantly "forget" its name, erase secret conversations, lock the doors, and pretend to be a broken iron. Protecting you matters more to it than keeping a couple of files.

---

## Why does any of this matter?

Of course, looking up a soup recipe or calculating a shop discount does not need this machinery.

TRANSFLUX is being built for things that matter more:

* **Medical devices**, where the cost of a mistake is a life, and the doctor must know how sure the device is of its readings.
* **Smart homes and robots**, which must understand the smoothness of change in the real physical world.
* **Safety systems** for people in dangerous situations, whose personal data must be protectable instantly and radically.

**In one line:** TRANSFLUX makes programs stop pretending they live in a perfect sterile world, and finally learn to work in the real one — with its uncertainty, its gradual change, and its dangers.
